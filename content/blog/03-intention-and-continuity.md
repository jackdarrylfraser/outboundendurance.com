Intention, dependent origination, and mind-stream bring the discussion back to
how actions shape experience.

<!-- more -->

## Intention matters

In the Buddhist accounts of karma that I have been trying to understand,
intention (_cetanā_) is central. That makes ethical reflection more specific
than tallying visible actions: it asks what we mean to do and how a choice is
being formed.

The first precept, for example, raises a difficult question for me about eating
meat. Buying meat can contribute to demand for the taking of life, but the
intention to buy a meal is not identical to the intention to kill in that
moment. Different Buddhist traditions may emphasize these parts of the question
differently. I find it useful to keep both the immediate intention and the wider
consequences in view.

## Dependent origination

Dependent origination takes the idea of conditions further. Things do not simply
arise from a single cause and then remain self-contained. What something is
depends on a web of conditions and relations; there is no need to posit an
unchanging substance behind that process.

For me, this gives a more precise direction to the earlier idea of
interdependence. Relationships are not just links between independently complete
things. Instead nothing in the web exists entirely different from anything else
and the relationships between each thing is what gives it it's defition.

This is a philosophical possibility, not something I can settle by naming it.
Questions about consciousness and other minds remain difficult. We recognize
other people as having inner experience, even though we do not directly access
their first-person perspective. That limit is one reason I want to keep
experience, humility, and careful inquiry together.

Across these three parts, the ideas I return to are connected: test teachings in
experience, treat descriptions as tools rather than fixed essences, and notice
how intention and conditions shape what follows. This is my developing
understanding, not a final map of Buddhism.

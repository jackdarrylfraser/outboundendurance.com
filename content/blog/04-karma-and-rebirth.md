Intention, dependent origination, and mind-stream bring the discussion back to
how actions shape experience.

<!-- more -->

This brings us to the ideas through which many people first encounter Buddhism:
karma as more than ordinary cause and effect, and rebirth after death.

When I pay attention to my experience, I find a kind of moment-to-moment
continuity that helps me understand the language of rebirth. Many of my thoughts
seemed to arise without my choosing them. When I tried to find the thought that
felt most like “me,” I was often drawn to the most emotional ones. Often before
I could even recognize a thought was happening, I was already attached to it;
carrying it out in day to day life or making plans to. It was impossible to
discern that I had not authored it.

Following that observation led me to question how much control I have over my
emotions. Many arise in response to conditions I did not choose. This made me
feel like I didn't have complete agency, and it made a fixed, independent self
harder to locate. Even the thoughts that feel most convincingly like “me” seem
to depend on causes and conditions that were verifiably not me.

The image of a person as a wave in the ocean helped me make sense of this. The
water molecules change, but the wave continues as a recognizable pattern. In the
same way, the person can be understood as a continuity through change rather
than as an unchanging essence. The wave eventually breaks on the shore, which
also makes the image a moving metaphor for death, decomposition, and the return
of our bodies to the wider processes of life.

This account fit my secular understanding of Buddhism, but I could feel
something was missing. There is nothing that it is like to be a wave in the
ocean. No first person subjective experience. The teaching of non-self had
already challenged some of my most basic assumptions; perhaps these teachings
called for the same openness.

Which bring to mind Dharmakīrti’s argument for rebirth:

1. For any entity or event to occur, its substantial cause (the primary
   ingredient or conditioning force) must share its essential nature.
2. Matter and consciousness possess completely different metaphysical
   characteristics. Matter has form, spatial dimensions, and lacks awareness,
   whereas consciousness is non-material, luminous, and inherently aware.
3. Because an effect must match the nature of its cause, matter cannot be the
   substantial cause of consciousness. Physical organs can act as a secondary
   condition (like a frame holding a canvas), but they cannot generate awareness
   itself.
4. Therefore, the very first moment of consciousness in a newborn infant or
   embryo cannot have been created by the physical body of the parents. It must
   have been caused by a preceding moment of consciousness. That preceding
   moment belongs to the final mind-moment of a previous life.

This four piece argument fits neatly to me into my mental model.

Science does not grant consciousness as immaterial and fundamental to the
universe.

To me I have no problem being open to karma/rebirth as a working hypothesis as
it seems to be a category error to suggest that science could
1st-person-subjective-expierence-ize itself into a 3rd person independently
verifiable objective procedure to create an inner experience.

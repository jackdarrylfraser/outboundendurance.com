The questions of non-self and interdependence lead from the experience of mind
into ethical ways of living.

<!-- more -->

In the first part, I described framings for how I find Buddhist teachings the
most acciesible rather than conclusions to accept on authority. Here I use those
framings as way to view buddhist concepts in a similar order to how they were
exposed to me when I was first learning them.

## The observer we expect to find

One of my first compelling encounters with Buddhist thought came from hearing
Sam Harris describe how meditation can make it possible to watch thoughts arise
and pass. If thoughts appear without being consciously chosen, the feeling of
being a separate person who authors every thought becomes harder to take for
granted.

This did not prove a complete theory of the mind to me. It did make the question
of self feel immediate. What is more central to our sense of existence than
awareness and the experience of being someone? The expierence that the observer
cannot be found as a separate thing among the experiences it observes felt both
completely impossible and yet having had the expierence of not intentionally
thinking my own thoughts it felt worth taking seriously.

Non-self, or _anattā_, is not a claim that people do not exist in any ordinary
sense. It challenges the idea of a permanent, independent self underneath
changing experience.

## Interdependence

The idea of interdependence extends the question beyond the individual. Causes
have effects, and what we call an effect depends on conditions that came before
it. The things we encounter do not stand wholly apart from those conditions or
from one another.

This way of thinking asks me to pay attention to relationships rather than
search for an isolated essence. It also makes the distinction between
conventional and ultimate description matter: we can name things in everyday
life while recognizing that those names do not reveal independent substances.

I also like the idea that you can see the reflection of Interdependence in
science. Independant 3rd party verification and cause and effect. Buddhism felt
like a process of understanding that could work with an emerging understaning of
cause and effect rather than needing a jumping off point somewhere which just
posits that supernatural events occur as a fundamental axiom.

I was curious to see how with this starting framework the more spiritual aspects
of Buddhism ( like rebirth and karma) could hold any real substance.

## Ethics as training

Before turning to the more spirtual aspects, buddhism asks that you use five
rules as training principles for daily actions. They are:

- refrain from taking life
- taking what is not given
- sexual misconduct
- false speech
- intoxicants that lead to heedlessness.

It's interesting to me to view these rules and the communication of these rules
in the light of expierential grounding and skillful means as opposed to divine
commands.

They invite questions about what our actions do, what habits they strengthen,
and whether they lead to less harm and less remorse. They make an account of
interdependence practical: what happens to others is not separate from how we
choose to live.
